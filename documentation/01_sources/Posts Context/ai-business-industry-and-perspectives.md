Mark Zuckerberg published an op-ed in the WSJ yesterday, “The AI Future Is for Everyone.” The framing of the piece is that the question about superintelligence is not whether it arrives but who ends up holding it.  

His case against concentrating it in a few institutions runs in three parts. 
1. Breakthroughs have historically come from outsiders rather than designated experts - the Wright brothers, Faraday, the garage PC etc. all came from people outside the institutional boundaries. 
2. More capable tools make individuals more able to shape the future rather than less. 
3. And there is no single objective best life since everyone’s experience and aspirations are relative, so no one system can be aligned to “humanity” in a way that serves everyone at once.   

He adds that betting on absolute power to benevolently provide for people has a poor historical record.  
 
While the first two bullets have gotten a lot of attention, I think the 3rd argument is super interesting. Alignment to humanity assumes humanity wants roughly the same things, which it does not, and a bigger model doesn’t resolve that. It’s a question about whose preferences get encoded. 

The outsider argument also matches what I see every week as an investor. Most of what I back comes from founders who are outside the “credentialed class” in their industry, working on problems that that industry had stopped considering interesting.

The entire piece is worth a read!

There are two GTM playbooks for AI companies selling into the enterprise: the Lighthouse and the Landgrab.

Two questions help you draw the map and decide which strategy you should pick:

How exposed is the buyer who signs?

Does social proof travel?

When exposure is high and proof travels, you're in lighthouse territory: a few credible buyers going first unlock the whole market.

When mistakes are recoverable and proof travels less, you're in landgrab territory: math closes the deal, and coverage wins the market.

If your buyer needs proof, go win the logo that gives it to them. If they need math, get on a plane and show it to them before your competitor or the incumbent does.

I read Satya Nadella's post this weekend on the reverse information paradox, and thought it was interesting. Every enterprise AI buyer should read it. 

His post's outline is straightforward - when you use a frontier model, you pay twice - once in dollars, once in knowledge. Your prompts, your agent workflows, your corrections when the model gets it wrong - all of it teaches the provider how your business actually works. He calls it the Reverse Information Paradox. In Arrow's original paradox, the seller of information risked giving it away. In AI, the buyer gives it away.

The fix? He tells enterprises to own the learning loop - private evals, tuning inside their own boundary, and an orchestration layer decoupled from any single model. He urges enterprises to be wary of using proprietary models as-is - “In consuming intelligence, you are creating intelligence. And what you create should belong to you”.

You can see this playing out in terms of who is building durable companies (in the enterprise context). Durable companies are building the layer where an enterprise's own learning accumulates - the evals, the routing, the memory, the audit trail.

I talk to engineers at other companies every day and hear the same thing: one person is 10x'ing their output with Claude but the rest of the org hasn't caught up.

Watching teams adopt AI, I keep seeing the same 4 steps. I mapped them out here: Steps of AI Adoption https://lnkd.in/ggWDMepq

There’s no one right path through the steps. Every team and company is different. But at each step, tokens aren’t enough to move you forward: to get to the next step, you need to find and break down the next set of bottlenecks, and build up the next set of guardrails.

In practice that means giving Claude ways to verify its own work end to end. It means enabling auto mode for permissions, defaulting on automated code review and security review, and using interfaces that let you manage multiple agents at once (Agent view in CLI, Desktop app, iOS and Android apps, Tag). To get to higher levels it means /loop, /batch, dynamic workflows, and worktree isolation for subagents. It's not about a single feature, but rather using the right features with the right guardrails that enable Claude to automate entire classes of work in a way that your team can trust the output.

Once your teams are bought in, how do you track it? Usage is worth watching (e.g. a dashboard), but it measures activity, not return. A better question: would you have spent engineering effort on this anyway? If yes, how much and what would it have cost in manual eng-hours? That's your return.

The bigger payoff comes when fixing and maintaining happens in the background and your teams can focus on building. That's when you start doing things that weren't even in range before.

Ever heard of MCP Apps?

MCP Apps is an extension of the Model Context Protocol (MCP) that enables chat interfaces to return rich, interactive visuals.

The classic examples are Booking.com, which would let ChatGPT or Claude directly show you hotel rooms to book from its chat interface, or Deliveroo, which would show you meals directly in the UI for you to confirm.

Various people believe MCP Apps will play a big role in the future, as text responses only get you so far. Sometimes users need to interact with data, not just read about it. MCP Apps let servers return interactive HTML interfaces (data visualizations, forms, dashboards) that render directly in the chat.

I'm the CTO of Wispr Flow, and I haven't written a line of code since December.

90% of the new code that serves 1M+ users is being written by AI.

In October, AI was a code assistant. By December, we were directing 5 to 10 fully autonomous coding agents at a time.

We barely touch execution anymore. Most of our engineering now comes down to four things:

1/ Problem definition.

I spend more time on what to build than how to build it. A clean, well-scoped problem turns into a working PR by the next morning. A fuzzy one burns through 50 agent-hours and lands nowhere.

2/ System architecture.

I decide what services exist, what they own, where state lives. If you get the architecture wrong, AI scales those mistakes faster than any human team could.

3/ QA.

I review AI-generated diffs the way I used to review my team's PRs. The model gets logic almost right. Almost right in production is a bug.

4/ Debugging.

AI can reproduce, hypothesize, and patch faster than any engineer I've worked with. But I'm still the one deciding whether the model actually understood the bug or just patched a symptom.

At first, it felt like I didn't have a job anymore. For weeks I'd open the editor out of muscle memory, realize I had nothing to type, and close it again.

But to work this way, I had to spend a decade and tens of thousands of hours writing code by hand.

Today I write almost no code. But I've never done more engineering in my life.

Last week in the Bay Area, I found myself sitting in rooms where some of the smartest founders in AI openly admitted the same thing: Nobody really knows where the world is headed.

And strangely, that was comforting.

Because beneath all the hype, noise, and AI hot takes, one thing became very clear: We are all entering a completely new era of company building.

Having spent one whole week with founders, operators, investors, and AI builders discussing what separates the companies that will survive from the ones that will disappear.

A lot of notes, debates, conviction, and honestly, a lot of uncertainty too.

Here are 5 insights that stayed with me:

1. The 95% Accuracy Threshold is the Magic Number: In vertical AI, 85% accuracy means customers spend 15% of their time fixing errors. At 95%, the flip happens and adoption scales rapidly. Every industry is waiting for this moment and whoever gets there first wins the vertical.

2. GTM is the New Moat, Not the Product: Code is essentially free now. Building a product is no longer a differentiator. What separates winners is distribution, forward deployed engineers, in-person relationships, and the ability to sell to strangers (not just your network). Your first $2M should not come from people you know.

3. Triple Your In-Person Meetings: This came up from almost every speaker. Enterprise deals above $100K are won face to face. Go to the Midwest, go to Iowa, go where no one else is going. The founders who are winning are the ones physically showing up where competitors are not.

4. Speed Over Everything. Ship Weekly, Not Quarterly: The companies that are thriving have completely abandoned quarterly roadmaps. Observe.ai went from shipping quarterly to weekly and called it their biggest unlock. Engineers talk directly to customers. No CPO, no layers. Bias to action is the new operating model.

5. Marry the Mission, Not the Plan: The AI landscape is shifting every 3 to 5 months. Products need to be rebuilt, teams need to change, and pricing models will evolve. The founders who are winning are the ones staying anchored to the problem they are solving, not the solution they originally built.

Hi, I am Aakriti Aggarwal, AI Researcher at IBM Research

Building fast that feels the same every single time is the hard part.

Every rider notices acceleration, but very few notice the work that goes into making it feel the same every single time.

Building a powerful electric motorcycle is a technical problem we solved fairly early.

Building one that delivers power identically at full charge and at 15%, in peak summer and in December, has taken far more of our engineering time, across motor control algorithms, battery management, thermal performance and traction calibration.

Any inconsistency, even for a fraction of a second, changes the rider's confidence.

The motorcycle can still be well within its performance limits and no longer feel completely connected to the person riding it.

The best riding experience is one where performance is predictable rather than surprising, where riders already know how the motorcycle will respond before they open the throttle.

That confidence is earned one ride at a time, every time the motorcycle behaves exactly as expected.

What earns your trust faster: a product that surprises you or one that never does?

---

People often ask why we chose to develop so much of our technology in-house.

The answer is simple. We didn't start with the intention of building everything ourselves.

We started by looking for existing components.

What we found were controllers designed for cars, battery management systems built for 48V and 72V scooters, and charging architectures that weren't meant for a high-voltage electric motorcycle.

The challenge is that these aren't independent components.

The motor controller shapes how the motorcycle accelerates, delivers power, and manages heat.

The battery management system determines how the battery performs, how long it lasts, and how safely it operates over thousands of charge cycles.

The charging system decides whether the motorcycle can access the growing CCS2 charging network that's already being built for electric cars.

Once those decisions are made, the platform is largely defined.

Building around off-the-shelf components would have meant designing our motorcycle around someone else's priorities and accepting the compromises that come with a low-voltage architecture.

So we built the controller, the BMS, and the charging system together as one integrated 650V platform.

It took more time. It demanded more engineering. But it gave us complete control over the riding experience, thermal performance, charging capability, and long-term reliability.

When you want to own the outcome, you have to own the technology that creates it.

---

Every engineering team eventually reaches a point where the numbers stop giving useful answers.

Performance targets are essential while building a motorcycle, and we have spent countless hours on torque curves, thermal limits, efficiency maps and power delivery, because every one of those measurements shapes the final product.

There comes a stage, though, when every graph looks acceptable and every test result falls within specification, yet the motorcycle still feels incomplete.

It performs exactly as intended on paper, but the riding experience says there is work left to do.

Those moments have led to some of our most useful engineering discussions, because they move the conversation past the data and back to the rider.

A motorcycle is never experienced as a spreadsheet, it is experienced through confidence, predictability and feel, and those do not always show up in the numbers.

The final stage of engineering usually begins where the measurable work ends.
